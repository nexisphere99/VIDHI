## LOCATION: HOSTEL ROOM 304

### NPC: MEERA   Chant Practice Session (Evening)

**[Trigger: Player interacts with Meera after 19:30, priya_out, swap_rules_established]**
**[SIDE QUEST: Practice the Swap Chant]**

Priya has left for her evening jog. The door is locked. The room is ours   twenty square meters of privacy, the rarest commodity in hostel life.

Meera sits on her bed, legs folded in padmasana   the lotus position, her Bharatanatyam training making the pose look effortless when I know from anatomy class that the hip rotation required is borderline orthopedic. She closes her eyes.

"The mantra has sixteen syllables," she says. "Om deha-vinimaya-siddhi-da namah Shivaya. Each syllable has to be precise   the dental consonants, the retroflex 'da', the aspirated 'dha' in deha. Sanskrit is a phonetic language. Mispronunciation doesn't just sound wrong   it IS wrong. The vibration pattern changes."

"Vibration pattern." I sit opposite her, mirroring her pose. My hips protest   I'm not a dancer, I'm a programmer, and the closest my body comes to flexibility is when I stretch to reach the Redmi under my mattress. "You sound like a physicist."

"Sanskrit phonetics IS physics. Each syllable generates a specific frequency. 108 repetitions create a resonance pattern. The kumkum circle acts as a " She pauses. Searches for a word. " a capacitor. It stores the resonance. The kalava thread is the circuit connecting two bodies."

"You've turned a fourteenth-century tantric ritual into an electrical engineering metaphor."

"I've been rooming with you for three years. Some things rub off."

She begins chanting. The syllables roll off her tongue   crisp, ancient, each consonant placed with the precision of a key struck on a keyboard. *Om deha-vinimaya-siddhi-da namah Shivaya.* The sound fills the room   not loud, but resonant, as if the stone walls are amplifying it, as if the building itself remembers Sanskrit from a time before it was a hostel.

I follow. My Sanskrit is functional   compulsory university paper, passing grade, the linguistic equivalent of driving an automatic when she's running manual. My "deha" is flat where hers curves. My "siddhi" has too much sibilance. My "Shivaya" sounds like I'm addressing a colleague, not a deity.

"Softer on the 'sh'," she corrects. "It's a palatal fricative, not an alveolar. The tongue should touch the hard palate, not the tooth ridge."

"Meera, I'm a medical student. I know exactly what the hard palate is. I just can't make my tongue do the right thing with it."

"Practice. Again."

We chant together for twenty minutes. Gradually my pronunciation smooths   the syllables finding grooves in my mouth, the rhythm syncing with Meera's, our voices layering. By repetition sixty, something happens   not the buzzing of the test swap, but a resonance. A feeling of the syllables fitting, like code compiling without errors, like a function returning the correct output.

"Better," Meera says. "Tomorrow, with Arjun, the three-person variation. I'll chant the anchor syllables while you two do the exchange mantra."

"Three-person?"

"I hold the circuit open while your consciousness and his pass through. Think of me as the server   you two are the data packets."

"You've definitely been rooming with me too long."

She smiles. In the dim room, with the agarbatti residue still sweetening the air and the mantra still echoing in the stone, Meera looks   powerful. Not the captive daughter, not the surveilled prisoner of Colonel Kulkarni's honor campaign. She looks like a woman who found a weapon in a library basement and is about to use it.

Priya bursts in sweating and chattering   the twin states of her existence. "Priya." I choose my next words carefully. "Tujhe nahi lagta ki yeh sab risky hai? What if Mrs. Joshi catches someone and reports it to their parents?"

"Risk toh hai. But what's the alternative? These girls haven't seen their boyfriends in months because the hostel rules are designed by people who think women should be locked in towers. So they find ways." She shrugs. "It's been happening since before we arrived. It'll happen after we leave."

She turns to Instagram. The intel session is over. But the data remains   a 30-minute window, a broken latch, a dark bathroom with a working bolt, and an unwritten code that protects exactly the kind of activity I'm about to engage in at a scale Anjali never imagined.

### SIDE QUEST: Last Coding Session as Kavya

**[Trigger: Player visits bj_library after 15:00]**

**[IMAGE: kavya_last_coding.jpg   INSERT HERE]**

The library computer section. Terminal 6. My terminal. The membrane keyboard that I've memorized down to the specific mushiness of each key, the particular delay on the spacebar, the way the Enter key requires a full press rather than a tap.

I sit. Open Notepad++. Pull up the Hindi tokenizer from yesterday. The GitHub issue assigned to KV_Codes this morning sits in my brain   Sanskrit loanword tokenization. The problem is nuanced: Hindi absorbs Sanskrit words but modifies their declension patterns. The tokenizer needs to recognize the root (dhatu) and distinguish between Sanskrit-origin compounds and pure Hindi formations.

My fingers begin.

```python
def tokenize_sanskrit_loanwords(text):
    # Sanskrit compound detection
    sandhi_patterns = load_sandhi_rules()
    for pattern in sandhi_patterns:
        if pattern.matches(text):
            # Split at sandhi junction
            components = pattern.split(text)
            return [tag_as_sanskrit(c) for c in components]
    return [tag_as_hindi(text)]
```

The code flows. The keyboard clicks. The monitor glows with the warm syntax highlighting of code that works. I'm not Kavya Iyer, MBBS student. I'm KV_Codes. The person who three professional developers praised. The person a maintainer wants on their core team.

Forty minutes. The tokenizer handles Sanskrit loanwords correctly   splitting compound words at sandhi junctions, tagging roots, preserving the morphological structure. I test it on a paragraph from the Bhagavad Gita and it performs beautifully   each word parsed, each compound separated, each Sanskrit root identified.

This is the last time I code as Kavya.

Tomorrow I code as Arjun. Same brain, different hands. A keyboard at VIT   probably better than this Dell atrocity. A monitor that doesn't flicker when someone on the floor above flushes a toilet. A chair that isn't designed as a torture device.

I save the file. USB drive. Clear history. Close Notepad++. Stand up.

The screen goes dark. My reflection in the black monitor   maroon kameez, wavy hair, dark circles, beauty mark. The face of a girl who codes in library basements and dissects cadavers and is about to swap bodies with a boy she's never met.

"Goodbye," I tell Terminal 6. "I'll be back. But I'll be different."

The terminal doesn't answer. It's a Dell desktop running Windows 7. But the sentiment stands.
