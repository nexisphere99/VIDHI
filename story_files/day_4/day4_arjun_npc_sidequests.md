## LOCATION: PHARMACOLOGY LECTURE HALL

### NPC: DR. MEENA PATWARDHAN   The Approachable Professor

**[Trigger: Pharmacology lecture, 10:00 AM]**

Dr. Patwardhan is the anti-Krishnan, the anti-Mhatre   where Krishnan is sharp and probing and Mhatre is stern and assessment-happy, Patwardhan is WARM. She teaches with the energy of a woman who genuinely enjoys pharmacology, which is a personality trait I didn't know existed until I attended her class.

"Drug interactions are conversations," she says, chalk in hand, a silver streak in her hair catching the fluorescent light. "Drug A talks to Enzyme B. Enzyme B stops talking to Drug C. Drug C accumulates in the blood and causes trouble. Understanding interactions is understanding RELATIONSHIPS."

Relationships. Conversations. She's teaching pharmacology through metaphor, and the metaphor lands because my brain processes relationships the same way it processes code   as interactions between entities with defined behaviors.

She writes on the board: *Warfarin + Aspirin = increased bleeding risk. Why?*

My hand doesn't go up. I've learned   from Krishnan's stunned reaction to Kavya-in-Arjun's brilliance   that answering too well is dangerous. But Patwardhan catches my eye.

"Kavya? Your thoughts?"

I stand. "Both drugs affect clotting. Warfarin inhibits vitamin K-dependent factors. Aspirin inhibits platelet aggregation via COX-1. Together, you're attacking coagulation from two angles   the cascade AND the cellular response. The bleeding risk isn't additive, it's synergistic."

Patwardhan beams. "Excellent. And notice the word SYNERGISTIC   the combined effect is greater than the sum of the parts. This is why we don't prescribe in isolation. Every drug is a conversation partner, and some conversations are dangerous."

I sit. The answer was accurate   accurate enough, drawing on Kavya's pharmacology knowledge (stored in the body's muscle memory of exam preparation) and my own logical framework. The synthesis worked. Nobody's eyebrows went up. Nobody's mouth fell open. The answer was KAVYA-LEVEL   competent, concise, medical. Not Arjun-level computer science, not Kavya-level coding genius. Just: a third-year MBBS student who knows her drug interactions.

The performance calibration is improving. I'm learning the difference between Kavya's register (medical, practical, understated) and my own (technical, enthusiastic, occasionally excessive). In this body, in this classroom, understatement is survival.
