### NPC: CHOWKIDAR RAMESH — Second Gate Pass

**[Trigger: Arjun-in-Kavya approaches hostel gate, ~6:00 AM]**

Ramesh's thermos is at half-capacity — the morning chai consumption progressing at its usual rate. He looks up from the crossword as I approach.

"Kavya didi, itni subah? Pooja se waapas?"

He KNOWS. Kavya signed out at 4:55 AM for temple pooja. Now she's returning at 6:00 AM. The timeline is consistent with a one-hour prayer visit. The cover holds without requiring additional explanation.

"Haan, Ramesh ji. Saat din ki pooja. Subah 5 baje."

"Acchi baat hai. Bhagwaan ka kaam." He nods with the approving energy of a man who considers early-morning religious devotion the gold standard of young-woman behavior. In the chowkidar's moral taxonomy, temple-going girls are the BEST category — devout, responsible, certainly not involved in anything that would require his reporting attention.

I sign in. *K. Iyer — 6:02 AM.* The handwriting — I'm getting better at rounding the letters, softening the angles. Not perfect Kavya-script, but close enough for a sign-in register that nobody scrutinizes.

### NPC: PRIYA — Phone Anomaly

**[Trigger: Morning, Room 304, ~6:45 AM, after Priya returns from yoga]**

Priya returns from her 7 AM yoga session at 7:45 — hair in a high ponytail, Lululemon-knockoff leggings, the specific glow of a woman who's spent forty-five minutes doing sun salutations and considers herself spiritually superior to everyone who hasn't.

She enters. Scans. The Priya radar — sweeping the room from left to right, pausing at each data point.

Kavya's bed: me, sitting, textbook open (Robbins Pathology, the eternal prop). This is FINE. This is what Kavya does.

Kavya's desk: the Redmi is not there. In its usual position — propped against the pencil holder, screen visible, notification light blinking — there's nothing. Because I'm not Kavya and I don't know that the Redmi lives on the desk during morning hours. I've put it under the pillow — my default phone storage, Arjun's habit translated into Kavya's space.

"Kavya, tera phone kahan hai?" Priya asks. Casual. Observational. But the question has TEETH — she's noticed the absence of a device that's been in the same position every morning for three years.

"Charge pe laga hua hai," I say. Quick. The Redmi DOES have a charging cable — it's plugged in beside the bed, under the pillow. The excuse works physically even if it violates Kavya's behavioral pattern.

"Tu aise charge karti hai? Usually desk pe rakhke charge karti hai." She says it lightly. But the observation is LOGGED. Another entry in Priya's running behavioral audit.

"Kal raat bed pe hi so gayi phone lekar."

She shrugs. Returns to Instagram. But the anomaly is filed — Kavya's phone not in its usual position. Small. Individually meaningless. Cumulatively building toward a conclusion Priya hasn't formed but is unconsciously approaching.

After she leaves for breakfast, I move the Redmi to the desk. The pencil holder. Propped at the angle I estimate from the dust outline. Note to self: ASK Kavya for a complete room-layout briefing. Every object's default position. Every habit's default execution. The small things are the dangerous things.

### NPC: MEERA — Anatomy Survival Guide

**[Trigger: Anatomy lab, 9:00 AM, Meera at adjacent table]**

**[IMAGE: arjun_anatomy_meera_guide.jpg — INSERT HERE]**

Meera is my lifeline in the dissection hall. She's positioned at the next table — close enough for whispered guidance, far enough that the proximity isn't unusual. Two roommates near each other in lab is unremarkable. Two roommates communicating through coded glances and micro-gestures is espionage.

The system: she looks at me. If my hand position is wrong — the scalpel angle, the depth of the cut, the plane of dissection — she touches her OWN hand in the correct position. I mirror. If I'm about to make a mistake she can see, she coughs once. If the mistake has already been made, she coughs twice.

Today's dissection: continued upper limb. The radial nerve — the same nerve I'll later discover I can dissect with unexpected precision, but that's Day 5. Today I'm working with the muscle attachments around the humerus, and Meera's coded guidance keeps me from cutting things that shouldn't be cut.

She coughs once. I look. She's touching her left hand with her right — thumb pressing the thenar eminence, the fleshy part below the thumb. The signal: I'm pressing too hard on the muscle I'm dissecting. I lighten the pressure. The tissue separates cleanly.

"Kavya, acchi technique hai," Dr. Sharma says, passing behind me. The compliment is Meera's — she directed every cut through the invisible language of coughs and touches and the specific telepathy of roommates who've developed their own TCP/IP protocol.

### NPC: SNEHA — Handwriting Notice

**[Trigger: Hostel corridor, 12:30 PM]**

Sneha intercepts me outside the mess with the focused energy of a woman on a academic mission.

"Kavya, tera anatomy notes dikha na — mujhe diagram chahiye, the brachial plexus one."

I hand her the notebook. She flips to the page — the brachial plexus diagram I copied from Meera's notebook yesterday, the labeled drawing of nerve roots, trunks, divisions, cords, branches.

She pauses. Studies the labels. The handwriting — MY handwriting attempting Kavya's script. I've been rounding letters, softening angles, but under stress (the anatomy lab, the scalpel, the formalin) my writing reverted to its default: angular, engineering-block, the precise lettering of a CS student who draws circuit diagrams.

"Kavya, teri handwriting aaj alag hai. Pehle se zyada neat — but different. Like someone else wrote it."

The ice in my veins could cool a data center. "New pen. Gel pen instead of ballpoint. It changes the stroke." I hold up the pen — a Cello Gripper, the common hostel pen. It IS a different pen from what Kavya usually uses (she uses a Pilot V5, fine tip, I know this from her desk inventory). The pen difference is coincidentally true and functionally saves me.

"Oh." Sneha examines the pen. Accepts. "Haan, gel pen se writing change hoti hai. Acchi lagi though — neater."

She photographs the diagram and leaves. The crisis passes — but the handwriting gap is a systemic vulnerability. I need to practice Kavya's script. Tonight. With the Pilot V5. Until the muscle memory forms in fingers that aren't mine.

**[sneha_handwriting: noticed, pen_excuse: deployed, sneha_suspicion: +1]**

### NPC: MESS DIDI SAVITA — The Observer

**[Trigger: Lunch, hostel mess, 12:45 PM]**

Savita ladles rice with the authority of a woman who has portioned food for two hundred girls and whose arm is calibrated to the gram. She adds dal — thin, yellow, the eternal hostel dal whose recipe hasn't changed since the institution was founded.

"Kavya! Aaj kuch zyada khush lag rahi hai." She squints at me through the steam. "Kya hua?"

"Kuch nahi, didi. Acchi neend aayi."

"Neend se chehra nahi chamakta. Ladka mila kya?"

The canteen laughter — two girls at the next table giggle. Mess didi's romantic radar is famous — she's called more crushes than any astrologer in Pune.

"Nahi didi, bas subah pooja ki. Temple gayi thi."

"Temple!" She ladles extra sabzi — the reward for religious devotion, which in mess didi's value system ranks just below eating everything on your plate. "Acchi baat hai. Bhagwaan se maango — accha ladka milega."

The irony. The boy I want is currently inside the body she's looking at. But mess didi's blessing is received with the gratitude it deserves — the warmth of institutional care, the surrogate mothering that feeds hostel girls when their real mothers are hours away.
